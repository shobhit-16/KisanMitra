# Indian Agriculture: Soil and Input Systems Analysis

## Executive Summary

India faces a compounding crisis across its agricultural foundation: soil health is degrading under imbalanced fertilizer use, water-scarce regions grow water-intensive crops, smallholder farmers face systemic information gaps, and hidden degradation (soil microbiome, micronutrients) threatens long-term productivity. The soil health card scheme provides data infrastructure but usage remains limited. The crisis is structural -- subsidy incentives, MSP signals, and risk aversion collectively lock farmers into unsustainable practices.

Complexity: **Complex** (Governance + Strategic + Legal dimensions)

---

## 1. Soil Health Crisis

### 1.1 Extent of Soil Degradation

India loses approximately **5.3 billion tonnes of soil** annually due to erosion, with about **146 million hectares** (45% of total geographic area) affected by various forms of degradation (ICAR, 2020).

**Types of degradation:**
| Type | Area Affected (Million Ha) | Primary Causes |
|------|---------------------------|----------------|
| Water erosion | 82.6 | Deforestation, overgrazing, improper cultivation |
| Wind erosion | 10.2 | Arid climate, desertification |
| Nutrient depletion | 9.8 | Imbalanced fertilizer use, inadequate replenishment |
| Waterlogging | 8.5 | Irrigation mismanagement |
| Salinization | 6.7 | Improper irrigation, groundwater rise |
| Alkali/Acid soils | 3.6 | Natural + anthropogenic factors |
| Mining/Industrial | 0.4 | Industrial discharge |

Source: National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning (NBSS&LUP), ICAR, 2020

### 1.2 NPK Imbalance

India's fertilizer consumption shows severe imbalance across N (Nitrogen), P (Phosphorus), K (Potassium):

**Fertilizer Consumption Ratio (N:P2O5:K2O):**

| Year | N | P | K | Ratio |
|------|-----|-----|-----|-------|
| 1970 | 47% | 31% | 22% | 2.1:1.4:1 |
| 1990 | 63% | 24% | 13% | 4.8:1.8:1 |
| 2010 | 72% | 22% | 6% | 12:3.7:1 |
| 2020 | 74% | 21% | 5% | 14.8:4.2:1 |
| 2023 | 76% | 20% | 4% | 19:5:1 |

Sources: Ministry of Chemicals & Fertilizers; FAOSTAT; IFFCO data

**The ideal ratio for most Indian soils is 4:2:1.** Current consumption ratio of 19:5:1 represents a nearly **5x excess of nitrogen** relative to potassium compared to optimal levels.

**State-wise imbalances:**
- Punjab, Haryana, Western UP: N:P:K ratios exceed 25:5:1 (extreme N bias)
- Coastal states: K deficiencies widespread due to leaching
- Central India: P deficiencies emerging as limiting factor

### 1.3 Urea Over-Dependence

India consumes approximately **35 million tonnes** of urea annually (2022-23), making it the **largest urea consumer globally** and **2nd largest nitrogen fertilizer importer** after USA.

**Urea as share of total N consumption:** ~85% of all nitrogen fertilizers applied in India is urea.

**Subsidy structure:** Urea is sold at a highly subsidized price of ~Rs 242/45kg bag (2023) while actual cost is ~Rs 2,500/bag. This creates severe price distortion:

- Farmers apply urea extensively, often at 2-4x recommended doses
- Incentive to overuse is structural -- urea is cheap, other fertilizers are relatively expensive
- Black market urea smuggling for industrial use (melamine, etc.) drains ~10-15% of subsidized supply

### 1.4 Declining Fertilizer Response Ratios

The yield gain per kilogram of fertilizer applied has declined significantly:

| Period | Rice (kg grain/kg N) | Wheat (kg grain/kg N) |
|--------|---------------------|----------------------|
| 1970s | 22-25 | 20-24 |
| 1980s | 18-20 | 16-18 |
| 1990s | 14-16 | 12-14 |
| 2000s | 10-12 | 9-11 |
| 2010s | 7-9 | 6-8 |
| 2020s | 5-7 | 4-6 |

Sources: IARI long-term fertilizer trials; IFPRI studies on nutrient use efficiency

**Partial Factor Productivity (PFP) of N in rice-wheat system:**
- 1970: ~80 kg grain per kg N applied
- 2020: ~15-18 kg grain per kg N applied
- **~80% decline in fertilizer efficiency over 50 years**

**Reasons:**
1. Mining of soil K reserves (no K applied for decades)
2. Reduced organic matter (crop residues burned, no manure recycling)
3. Decline in legume area (fixed N not replenished)
4. Light-textured soils with high leaching losses

## 2. Crop-Geography Mismatch

### 2.1 Water-Intensive Crops in Water-Scarce Regions

**Punjab -- The Groundwater Crisis:**

Punjab exemplifies the crop-water mismatch. The state grows:
- **Rice** on ~3.1 million hectares (32% of cropped area)
- **Wheat** on ~3.5 million hectares (35% of cropped area)

**Water requirements:**
- Rice: 1,500-2,000 mm (predominantly paddy with flood irrigation)
- Wheat: 450-600 mm
- Punjab receives only 700-1,100 mm rainfall annually

**Aquifer depletion rates:**

| Zone | Decline Rate (cm/year) | Status |
|------|------------------------|--------|
| Central Punjab (Ludhiana, Moga, Ferozepur) | 30-50 cm/year | Critical |
| Southwest Punjab (Bathinda, Muktsar, Fazilka) | 50-80 cm/year | Severe |
| Eastern Punjab (Ropar, Mohali, Patiala) | 15-25 cm/year | Moderate |
| State Average | 35-40 cm/year | Critical |

Source: Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) Punjab Report, 2022

**Net groundwater draft:** ~18.5 billion cubic meters/year
**Net annual recharge:** ~7.5 billion cubic meters/year
**Deficit:** ~11 billion cubic meters/year (60% deficit)

Punjab will exhaust accessible groundwater reserves within **25-30 years** at current extraction rates.

**Haryana groundwater crisis:**

Similar pattern, with 20-45 cm/year decline in most blocks. Haryana's rice area has expanded to ~1.5 million ha despite receiving only 400-500 mm rainfall in most districts.

### 2.2 Displacement of Millets and Traditional Crops

**Area shifts (million hectares):**

| Crop | 1960 | 1990 | 2020 | Change |
|------|------|------|------|--------|
| Rice | 30.8 | 42.2 | 46.4 | +51% |
| Wheat | 12.9 | 23.8 | 31.5 | +144% |
| Finger Millet | 2.5 | 1.8 | 1.1 | -56% |
| Pearl Millet (Bajra) | 11.4 | 10.0 | 7.4 | -35% |
| Sorghum (Jowar) | 17.6 | 11.0 | 4.7 | -73% |
| Small Millets | 3.2 | 1.4 | 0.4 | -87% |

Sources: FAO; Ministry of Agriculture; ICAR-IIMR reports

**Nutritional displacement impact:**

Millets are 2-3x more protein-dense than rice, high in iron, zinc, calcium, and dietary fiber. Their displacement correlates with:

- India's **64% anemia prevalence** among women (NFHS-5, 2019-21)
- **34.7%** stunting in children under 5 (NFHS-5)
- Rising type-2 diabetes (current: ~77 million cases, projected 134 million by 2045)

### 2.3 Protein Deficiency and Dietary Shifts

**Protein consumption data:**
- Average Indian protein intake: ~48g/day (recommended: 60g/day)
- Rural: 43g/day
- Urban: 53g/day
- Target under National Nutrition Mission: 55g/day by 2025

**Pulses -- the protein backbone -- declining:**
- Area: 23.7 million ha (1960) → 15.0 million ha (2020), -37%
- Production growth unable to keep pace with population
- India imports ~4-5 million tonnes of pulses annually

**Dietary energy supply shift:**
- Cereals (rice/wheat) now provide 50-55% of dietary energy
- Pulses: 7-8% (target: 10-12%)
- Vegetables/Fruits: 6-8% (target: 15-20%)
- Animal products: 8-10% (target: 15-20%)

## 3. Input Efficiency Problems

### 3.1 Mechanization Gaps and Custom Hiring Centers

**Farm mechanization status:**
- Overall farm mechanization level: ~40-45% (vs. 90%+ in developed nations)
- Availability of suitable tractors: 77 per 1,000 ha
- Combine harvesters: 4 per 1,000 ha
- Reapers: 7 per 1,000 ha
- Threshers: 42 per 1,000 ha

**Custom Hiring Centers (CHCs):**
Government scheme launched 2015-16 under **SMAM (Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanization)**:
- Target: Establish 1 CHC per block (6,841 blocks nationally)
- Achievement (as of 2022): ~3,800 functional CHCs operational
- Gap: ~44% coverage shortfall

**Key challenges:**
- CHCs primarily serve large farmers (own 5+ ha)
- Smallholders (85% of farmers, <2 ha) cannot afford CHC rates
- Harvesting costs for paddy: Rs 2,500-3,500/acre (often prohibitive for marginal farmers)
- Machinery breakdown leads to labor bottlenecks during peak season
- Suboptimal scheduling causes harvest losses of 10-15% in wheat

### 3.2 Seed Replacement Rates

**Seed replacement rate (SRR):** Percentage of cropped area sown with certified/quality seeds instead of farm-saved seed.

| Crop | Recommended SRR | Actual SRR (2022) | Gap |
|------|----------------|-------------------|-----|
| Rice | 100% | 50-55% | Critical |
| Wheat | 100% | 65-70% | Major |
| Maize | 100% | 60-65% | Major |
| Cotton | 100% | 75-80% | Moderate |
| Pulses | 100% | 25-30% | Severe |

Sources: Ministry of Agriculture; ICAR-IIVR reports

**Seed quality issues:**
- ~Rs 3,500 crore annual market for spurious seeds (estimated)
- Seed certification coverage: Only 25-30% of total seed used
- Informal seed saving and exchange dominates marginal farm practices
- Release of new varieties not reaching small farmers (extension gap)

**Quality seed availability gap:**
- Total requirement: ~55 lakh quintals for rice alone
- Quality seed availability: ~42 lakh quintals
- Gap: ~24% shortfall

### 3.3 Pesticide Overuse and Resistance

**Pesticide consumption:**
- India: ~60,000 tonnes active ingredient/year (2021-22)
- Per hectare use: 0.5 kg/ha (low globally) but concentrated in target crops
- Punjab: 4-6 kg/ha (one of highest globally for food grains)

**IPM (Integrated Pest Management) adoption:**
- Target: 50% of area under IPM
- Actual achievement: ~15-18% of cropped area
- IPM adoption in Punjab: Only 5-7% despite being the first state to launch IPM in 1991

**Resistance development:**
- Brown planthopper (Nilaparvata lugens) in rice: High resistance to imidacloprid, cartap
- Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) in cotton: Resistance to pyrethroids, organophosphates
- Rice blast pathogen: Reduced sensitivity to tricyclazole in several states
- Diamondback moth: Resistance to all major insecticide classes

**Health impacts:**
- Acute pesticide poisoning: ~10,000-15,000 deaths/year reported
- Actual burden estimated 5-10x higher (underreporting)
- Pesticide residue in food: 10-15% samples violate FSSAI limits (2021-22)

### 3.4 Water Use Efficiency

**Irrigation efficiency in India:**
- Overall irrigation efficiency: 35-38%
- Canal systems: 45-50%
- Drip/Micro-irrigation: 70-85%
- Flood irrigation (majority): 30-35%

**Target vs Achievement for Micro-Irrigation:**

| Parameter | Target (2020) | Achievement |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| Micro-irrigation area | 10 million ha | 7.3 million ha (73%) |
| State-wise leaders | -- | Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka |
| Lagging states | -- | Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu |

Source: Ministry of Agriculture, 2022

**Crop-wise water productivity (kg grain/mm water):**

| Crop | 1980s | 2010s | Improvement |
|------|-------|-------|-------------|
| Rice | 7-8 | 12-15 | +70% |
| Wheat | 10-12 | 15-18 | +50% |
| Maize | 12-15 | 18-22 | +55% |
| Cotton | 3-4 | 5-7 | +75% |

Despite improvements, India's water productivity remains **50-60% lower** than developed nations due to:
- Flood irrigation dominance
- Free electricity to farmers (no incentive to conserve)
- Damaged canal infrastructure
- Lack of pressurized irrigation systems

## 4. Information Gaps

### 4.1 Why Farmers Don't Diversify

**Risk aversion framework:**

Behavioral studies (IFPRI, 2019) show:
- 73% of smallholders cite "income uncertainty" as primary reason for non-diversification
- 62% cite "lack of assured marketing" 
- 58% cite "fear of price crash" in new crops
- 45% cite "non-availability of procurement" for alternate crops

**MSP (Minimum Support Price) signal distortion:**

MSP announced for 23 crops but **procurement infrastructure** highly skewed:
- Wheat and Rice: 85-90% procured at MSP (government dominates)
- Pulses: 20-25% procured at MSP
- Oilseeds: 15-20% procured at MSP
- Millets: <5% procured at MSP (nearly absent from MSP system)

This creates perverse incentive: farmers rationally choose crops with guaranteed MSP uptake (rice/wheat) over potentially more profitable but risky alternatives.

**Consumption pattern lock-in:**
- Wheat/rice are culturally preferred, socially expected
- Rice straw/wheat stubble used for fodder, fuel, thatching
- Pulses require processing infrastructure lacking in rural areas
- Millets require different processing (dehusking, milling) -- market chain underdeveloped

### 4.2 Soil Health Card Scheme

**Scheme overview:**
- Launched 2015 by Ministry of Agriculture
- Target: Issue soil health cards to all 120 million farm holdings
- Card contains: 12 parameters (N, P, K, pH, EC, OC, S, Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, B)

**Achievement (as of 2023):**
- Cards issued: ~230 million (covers ~115% of farm holdings -- multiple cycles)
- District coverage: 686 districts covered
- Cycles completed: 2 full cycles + ongoing 3rd cycle

**However, behavioral impact is minimal:**

| Indicator | Finding |
|-----------|---------|
| Farmers reading SHC fully | ~30-35% |
| Farmers following SHC recommendations | ~15-18% |
| Input purchase change based on SHC | ~12-15% |
| Yield improvement attributed to SHC | ~8-10% |

Sources: NITI Aayog evaluation, 2021; IARI impact studies

**Why SHC hasn't changed behavior:**
1. **Language/education barrier:** Cards issued in state language, technical terms unexplained
2. **Lack of input linkage:** Card says "low K" but fertilizer shop doesn't sell K, or K fertilizer 3x price of urea
3. **Timing mismatch:** Cards issued post-harvest, recommendations for next season arrive after input decisions made
4. **Extension worker gap:** 1 agricultural extension officer per 1,000+ farmers (vs. 1:400 target)
5. **No digital integration:** Data stays on paper; no AI/telephony interface for illiterate farmers
6. **Social learning gap:** Success stories of SHC adopters not disseminated

### 4.3 Crop Suitability Mapping

**Available data:**
- ICAR's **Crop Explorer Portal** provides district-level suitability maps
- NBSS&LUP has **Land Resource Inventory** for 92% of villages
- State-level remote sensing data from ISRO (Sujala, Bhuvan)

**But farmers don't use this data because:**

| Barrier | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Awareness gap | 85%+ farmers unaware of these portals |
| Digital literacy | <20% of smallholders can access web portals |
| Relevance question | District/block level too coarse for farm decisions |
| Language | Portals not in local languages |
| Actionability | Maps don't tell farmers WHAT to grow instead |
| Credit linkage | Crop change requires bank financing; banks don't accept map recommendations |
| Insurance gap | New crops not covered under PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana) |

**Agristack initiative:**
- Launched 2021 as digital stack for agriculture
- Components: FMIS (Farmer Machine ID), soil health registry, crop registry, land records
- Status: Pilot in 6 states; full rollout delayed due to consent framework issues
- Farmers issued: ~50 million farmer IDs (as of 2023) vs. 120 million target

## 5. Hidden Issues

### 5.1 Soil Microbiome Degradation

**The unseen crisis:**

Healthy soil contains ~**10^8-10^9 bacteria, 10^5-10^6 fungi** per gram. This microbiome:
- Fixes atmospheric N (biological nitrogen fixation)
- Solubilizes rock phosphate (P availability)
- Produces plant growth hormones
- Suppresses soil-borne pathogens

**Indian soil microbiome status:**

| Indicator | Optimal | Current Indian Status |
|-----------|---------|----------------------|
| Organic carbon | >0.75% | 0.3-0.5% (most regions) |
| Microbial biomass C | >500 ug/g | 150-250 ug/g |
| Dehydrogenase activity | >30 ug TPF/g/hr | 8-15 ug TPF/g/hr |
| Urease activity | >25 ug N/g/hr | 6-12 ug N/g/hr |

Source: ICAR-IISS, Bhopal long-term studies

**Causes:**
1. Zero or minimal fallow periods
2. Residue burning (removes organic matter, kills microbes)
3. Excessive tillage (destroys fungal networks)
4. Fumonisin and pesticide residues
5. Chemical NPK (kills mycorrhizal fungi)

**Productivity impact:** Estimated 20-40% of yield reduction in intensive systems attributable to microbiome degradation (IARI estimates).

### 5.2 Micronutrient Deficiencies

**Prevalence across Indian soils (2022 assessment):**

| Micronutrient | Deficient Soils (%) | Most Affected States |
|--------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Zinc (Zn) | 48% | Punjab, Haryana, UP, Bihar, West Bengal |
| Iron (Fe) | 12% | Calcareous soils, Bihar, Odisha |
| Manganese (Mn) | 6% | Limited pockets |
| Boron (B) | 23% | Rajasthan, Gujarat, Odisha, West Bengal |
| Copper (Cu) | 4% | Isolated pockets |

Source: IISS Micronutrient Map, 2022; ICAR research papers

**Multi-micronutrient deficiency:**
- ~65% of India's soils show deficiency in **at least one micronutrient**
- ~25% show deficiency in **2 or more micronutrients**
- Zinc deficiency alone estimated to cause **40 million tonne** loss in foodgrains annually

**Economic impact:**
- Micronutrient deficiencies cost India ~**$12-15 billion/year** in lost agricultural output (World Bank estimate)
- Correction cost: ~$1-2 billion/year (relatively cheap intervention)

### 5.3 Fertilizer Subsidy Structure Incentivizing Imbalance

**Subsidy architecture (2023-24):**

| Fertilizer Type | Subsidized Price (Rs/45kg) | Actual Cost | Subsidy per bag |
|-----------------|---------------------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Urea | 242 | 2,500 | 2,258 |
| DAP | 1,350 | 2,800 | 1,450 |
| MOP | 1,665 | 2,400 | 735 |
| NPK (Complex) | 1,500-1,800 | 2,200-2,600 | 700-800 |

Source: Ministry of Chemicals & Fertilizers, 2023

**Distortions created:**
1. **Urea is cheapest per kg of N:** Rs 5.4/kg N vs. DAP at Rs 13.1/kg N
2. **K effectively penalized:** MOP is ~Rs 23/kg K, but urea is Rs 5.4/kg N -- incentive to skip K
3. **Subsidy on DAP masks P cost:** Makes P appear cheap relative to K
4. **Nutrient-based subsidy (NBS) introduced 2010 but:** Not implemented uniformly; urea still controlled

**Fiscal burden:**
- Total fertilizer subsidy 2023-24: ~Rs 2.5 lakh crore (~$30 billion)
- Urea subsidy: ~Rs 1.8 lakh crore (72% of total)
- But agricultural output per Rs subsidy declining: 1990: 15 kg grain per Rs 100 subsidy; 2023: 4 kg grain per Rs 100 subsidy

### 6.2 Root Cause Analysis (5-Why)

**Problem: Soil degradation + crop-water mismatch in Punjab**

Why 1: Farmers grow rice-wheat despite water scarcity
> Because MSP guarantees rice/wheat purchase; alternate crops lack procurement

Why 2: Alternate crops lack procurement
> Because government procurement infrastructure built for rice/wheat (food self-sufficiency narrative)

Why 3: Rice/wheat MSP dominates procurement
> Because political economy -- rice-wheat states (Punjab, Haryana, UP) have political influence

Why 4: Fertilizer subsidy skewed to urea
> Because urea manufacturing (NFL, NFL, HMT) employs thousands; subsidy preserves votes

Why 5: (Root) -- No political incentive to change input subsidies or procurement patterns
> Because the current system benefits politically significant farmer constituencies AND food inflation control

**Structural fix required at policy level, not farmer level.**

## 8. Recommendations for Digital Product Intervention Points

Based on the above analysis, the following intervention points are identified for a digital agriculture product:

### High-Impact / Feasible
1. **Soil health card interpretation layer** -- Translate SHC data into actionable recommendations via IVR/SMS
2. **Crop suitability push notifications** -- Personalized advice based on farmer's plot, soil, weather
3. **Market price intelligence** -- Real-time mandi prices + MSP procurement center mapping
4. **Input shop discovery** -- GPS-tagged fertilizer/pesticide shops with stock availability

### Medium-Impact / Requires Partnership
5. **Crop diversification incentive tracking** -- Tie SHC compliance to input subsidies
6. **CHC scheduling platform** -- Aggregate demand for machinery hire
7. **Pesticide resistance alerts** -- Heat map of resistance patterns by region
8. **Groundwater usage benchmarking** -- Peer comparison of water use efficiency

### High-Impact / Long-Term
9. **Farmer producer organization (FPO) linkage** -- Collective marketing for diversified crops
10. **Credit scoring based on land health** -- Soil health as collateral for input loans
11. **Insurance product for diversified crops** -- Weather-indexed insurance for non-MSP crops
12. **Input-output traceability** -- QR-based fertilizer tracking to reduce black market
